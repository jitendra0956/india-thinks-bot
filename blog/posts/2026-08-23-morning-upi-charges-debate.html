UPI Charges: Balancing Sustainability and Financial Inclusion

August 23, 2026 · 3 min read

Parliament recently passed a bill on August 10, 2026, making it legally possible to levy charges on Unified Payments Interface (UPI) transactions. This development has reignited a crucial national debate: how to ensure the long-term sustainability of India's revolutionary digital payment system without undermining its widespread adoption and impact on financial inclusion. While UPI has transformed how millions transact, the underlying infrastructure incurs significant costs, prompting a re-evaluation of its funding model.

Why people say yes

Proponents argue that charging for UPI transactions is essential for the ecosystem's long-term viability. Operating India's massive UPI infrastructure is not free; banks, the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI), and other payment companies incur substantial costs for maintaining technology, settlement systems, cybersecurity, and fraud detection. Introducing a charge would help cover these operational expenses, which are currently largely absorbed by stakeholders and government subsidies.

Furthermore, a sustainable revenue stream would enable continuous investment in innovation and infrastructure expansion. As UPI aims for even higher transaction volumes and new features like voice onboarding and advanced fraud detection, significant funding is required. Relying solely on government incentives or banks to absorb costs might hinder future technological advancements and the overall security of the platform. A user-pays model, even a partial one, could secure the necessary capital for growth.

Many advocating for charges suggest a targeted approach to minimize impact on everyday users. Current proposals often focus on levying a Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) on higher-value merchant transactions, typically above a certain threshold like ₹2,000, while keeping person-to-person (P2P) and small-value consumer-to-merchant (P2M) payments free. This method aims to generate revenue from commercial transactions where businesses might have higher margins, without burdening the common consumer for small payments.

Why people say no

Opponents vehemently argue that introducing charges would undermine the very foundation of UPI's success: its accessibility and zero-cost model. UPI has been instrumental in driving financial inclusion across India, bridging the gap for millions of unbanked and underbanked individuals by offering a simple, free, and efficient digital payment method. Imposing fees could reverse these gains, making digital payments less attractive, especially for low-income users and those in rural areas.

Even a nominal charge could significantly burden small merchants who operate on thin margins. With 86% of merchant payments below ₹500, a small percentage fee could have a disproportionate impact on their profitability. There is also a concern that merchants might pass these costs on to consumers through increased prices, thereby affecting the general public despite government assurances that consumers won't directly pay. This could erode trust in the system and hinder the 'ease of doing business'.

Another significant concern is the potential for users to revert to cash. If digital payments become more expensive or complex due to charges, many consumers and small businesses might opt for cash transactions again. This would negate years of effort to formalize the economy and reduce dependency on physical currency, which itself incurs costs for the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) in terms of printing and logistics. Maintaining a free UPI is seen by many as a public good, similar to roads or streetlights, facilitating economic activity.

Key facts

  • Running India's UPI infrastructure costs an estimated ₹20,000 crore annually, a cost shared between banks, NPCI, and payment service providers.
  • In the financial year 2025-26, UPI processed over 24,000 crore transactions, with approximately 86% of merchant payments being below ₹500.
  • While Parliament has made it legally possible to charge, the government has stated that person-to-person and most low-value consumer-to-merchant UPI payments will continue to remain free for users.

The bottom line

The debate over charging for UPI transactions highlights a fundamental tension between maintaining a universally accessible, free digital public good and ensuring its long-term financial health. As India’s digital payment ecosystem continues its exponential growth, finding a balanced revenue model that supports infrastructure and innovation without alienating its vast user base or hindering financial inclusion remains a complex challenge for policymakers and stakeholders alike.
