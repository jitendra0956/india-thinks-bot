UPI Charges: Balancing Innovation, Access, and Sustainability in India's Digital Payments

August 19, 2026 · 3 min read

India's Unified Payments Interface (UPI) has been a global success story, revolutionizing digital transactions with its ease of use and zero-cost model for most users. However, a significant legislative development occurred recently: the Lok Sabha passed a bill on August 6, 2026, which received presidential assent on August 17, 2026, authorizing the government to permit banks to levy charges on specified UPI transactions. This move has ignited a nationwide debate about the future of digital payments, aiming to address the long-standing question of who should bear the costs of maintaining this massive, free-to-use infrastructure.

Why people say yes

Proponents argue that allowing banks to charge for certain UPI transactions is crucial for the long-term sustainability and growth of the digital payments ecosystem. Banks and payment service providers incur substantial operational costs, including maintaining infrastructure, ensuring robust cybersecurity, detecting fraud, and handling transaction reconciliation and settlement. Currently, with zero Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) on most UPI transactions, these costs have been largely absorbed by the institutions and partially subsidized by the government, a model deemed unsustainable by many industry players. Introducing charges, particularly on high-value merchant transactions, would create a direct revenue stream, enabling continuous investment in technology, innovation, and enhanced security measures.

Furthermore, the current free model has put pressure on traditional bank revenue streams, unlike card payments which generate transaction-linked income. The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) Governor, Sanjay Malhotra, has also stated the fundamental economic principle that "somebody has to pay" for the services provided by UPI. A calibrated approach, focusing on larger transactions and merchants, could ensure the ecosystem remains financially viable while protecting small businesses and individual users. This could also address concerns from international card networks regarding an uneven playing field.

Why people say no

Critics strongly advocate for UPI to remain a free public good, arguing that introducing charges would undermine its core benefits and widespread adoption. UPI is often likened to essential public utilities like roads or streetlights, whose societal benefits outweigh direct user charges. The fear is that even nominal fees could deter users, especially small merchants and low-income individuals, potentially pushing them back towards cash transactions. This regression could reverse the significant progress made in financial inclusion and the formalization of the economy.

Moreover, imposing charges on merchants could lead to increased logistics costs and reduced profitability for small businesses, who might, in turn, pass these costs onto consumers through higher prices. This would negate the "ease of doing business" advantage that free UPI has provided. Opponents also point out that the government already allocates funds to subsidize the UPI ecosystem, meaning taxpayers are already contributing to its upkeep. They argue that UPI has actually reduced costs for banks by decreasing the need for cash handling, ATM networks, and cheque processing, and has saved the RBI considerable sums on printing currency.

Key facts

  • UPI is recognized as the world's largest real-time payment system.
  • In July 2026, UPI processed 23.66 billion transactions valued at ₹29.88 trillion.
  • The costs to operate the UPI ecosystem are estimated to be as high as ₹20,000 crore annually.

The bottom line

The government has clarified that person-to-person (P2P) and the vast majority of consumer-to-merchant (P2M) UPI transactions will continue to be free for users. The proposed charges, known as Merchant Discount Rate (MDR), are primarily being considered for high-value merchant transactions, potentially those exceeding ₹2,000 and involving larger businesses. While the exact fee structure and thresholds are still being finalized, this legislative change marks a pivotal moment in India's digital payments journey, aiming to strike a balance between fostering innovation, ensuring widespread accessibility, and achieving financial sustainability for this vital public infrastructure.
