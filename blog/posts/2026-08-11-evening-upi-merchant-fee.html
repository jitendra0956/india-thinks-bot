Decoding UPI: Should Merchants Pay Fees on High-Value Transactions?

August 11, 2026 · 3 min read

India's digital payments landscape is at a crossroads, sparked by the Finance Ministry's introduction of the Payment and Settlement Systems (Amendment) Bill, 2027 on August 4, 2026. This pivotal bill removes the statutory bar on charging Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) on UPI/RuPay transactions, opening up a fervent debate: Should merchants be required to pay a fee for UPI transactions exceeding ₹2,000? This move signals a potential shift from the long-standing zero-MDR policy, aimed at fostering a more sustainable digital payments ecosystem, but it also raises concerns about its impact on businesses and consumers.

Why people say yes

Proponents argue that introducing a nominal MDR for high-value UPI transactions is crucial for the long-term sustainability and growth of India's digital payments infrastructure. The Unified Payments Interface (UPI) processes billions of transactions monthly, requiring significant, continuous investment in cybersecurity, fraud prevention, and technological upgrades. The current model, heavily reliant on government subsidies, is deemed unsustainable and insufficient to cover these escalating operational costs. A dedicated revenue stream from MDR could ensure UPI remains robust, inclusive, and future-ready.

Furthermore, a viable revenue model derived from MDR could incentivize greater private sector participation and investment in the digital payments space. Banks, payment aggregators, and fintech companies currently incur substantial costs for authentication, dispute resolution, and maintaining the payment plumbing without direct transaction-based revenue. Providing a clear financial incentive could foster healthy competition and accelerate innovation, leading to enhanced services and broader adoption of digital payment solutions across the country.

Many also argue for fair cost allocation, especially for larger merchants. Businesses, particularly those with high transaction volumes, derive significant benefits from accepting UPI payments, including expanded customer reach, reduced cash handling expenses, and streamlined reconciliation processes. It's seen as equitable for these beneficiaries to contribute to the maintenance and enhancement of the infrastructure that facilitates their sales. The proposed MDR is expected to be nominal, potentially ranging from 0.3% to 0.5%, significantly lower than existing debit and credit card MDRs.

Why people say no

Opponents express strong concerns about the potential negative impact on businesses, particularly small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), even with a threshold. While the proposal aims to target high-value transactions or larger merchants, even a nominal fee can significantly erode already thin profit margins for many businesses. This could compel merchants to either absorb the cost, leading to financial strain, or pass it on to consumers through increased product prices, indirectly burdening the public.

The zero-MDR policy, implemented since January 2020, has been a cornerstone of UPI's unprecedented success and its role in fostering financial inclusion across India. Reintroducing any fee, even on high-value transactions, risks creating friction and discouraging merchants from adopting or promoting UPI, potentially slowing down the momentum of digital payment adoption that has been a hallmark of India's digital transformation. Small merchants, who previously shunned card payments due to MDR, embraced UPI precisely because it was free.

Furthermore, the implementation of a transaction-value or turnover-based MDR could introduce significant complexity and compliance burdens for merchants. Differentiating between free and chargeable transactions, managing new accounting requirements, and resolving potential disputes would add administrative overhead, contradicting the government's stated goal of simplifying the 'ease of doing business'. Many argue that if UPI is considered a public good essential for formalizing the economy, the government should fully underwrite its costs rather than relying on merchants or an unpredictable subsidy model.

Key facts

  • UPI recorded 23.6 billion transactions worth ₹29.9 trillion in July 2026, solidifying its position as one of the world's largest real-time payment systems.
  • The government mandated zero Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) on UPI and RuPay debit card transactions from January 2020 to boost digital payment adoption.
  • The Finance Ministry has clarified that consumers will not face any direct charges for UPI transactions, and Person-to-Person (P2P) and most small merchant transactions will remain free.

The bottom line

The debate over merchant fees on UPI transactions above ₹2,000 highlights a critical tension between fostering a self-sustaining digital payments ecosystem and ensuring continued inclusive growth. While a revenue model could bolster infrastructure and innovation, concerns remain about the potential impact on merchant profitability and the risk of slowing digital adoption. The ultimate decision, to be shaped by the UPI and Services Steering Committee, will be pivotal in defining the future trajectory of India's dynamic digital economy.
