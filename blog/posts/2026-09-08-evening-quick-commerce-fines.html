Expired Food on Your Doorstep: Should Quick-Commerce Fines Get Tougher?

September 08, 2026 · 3 min read

In recent months, India's quick-commerce sector, lauded for its rapid delivery services, has come under intense scrutiny. Following a surge of consumer complaints in July and August 2026 concerning expired, rotten, and contaminated food, the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) intensified its inspections of food businesses as of September 2, 2026. This crackdown has sparked a nationwide debate: should quick-commerce platforms face significantly higher fines for food safety violations?

Why people say yes

Advocates for stricter penalties argue that higher fines are crucial for safeguarding public health. The delivery of expired or contaminated food poses serious health risks to consumers, some of whom have reported receiving rotten eggs or unsafe infant food formulations. Increased fines would serve as a stronger deterrent, compelling quick-commerce platforms to invest more rigorously in quality control, proper storage, and timely inventory management.

Furthermore, proponents emphasize the need for greater accountability from these rapidly growing platforms. While quick-commerce companies act as Food Business Operators (FBOs) and are jointly liable with manufacturers, existing fines, which can go up to ₹5 lakh for selling unsafe food, might not be sufficient to deter large corporations. Higher penalties would ensure these platforms take full responsibility for the products delivered to consumers' doorsteps, fostering a culture of compliance and trust.

Finally, higher fines could drive much-needed systemic changes. Recent inspections have revealed lapses such as cockroach infestations, expired inventory, and inadequate cold storage in quick-commerce 'dark stores'. By raising the financial stakes, regulators aim to force platforms to overhaul their operational protocols, hire more quality checkers, and ensure robust food safety management systems are in place from warehouse to consumer.

Why people say no

Conversely, opponents of higher fines argue that such measures could disproportionately impact the quick-commerce industry and, by extension, consumers. Quick-commerce platforms already operate with high logistical costs to maintain their rapid delivery promises. Imposing significantly larger fines could lead to increased operational expenses, which might then be passed on to consumers through higher prices or additional service fees.

Another concern is the potential burden on the numerous small and medium-sized vendors that rely on these quick-commerce platforms. While the platforms are FBOs, many products are sourced from a vast network of suppliers. Drastically increased fines could create an environment where platforms become overly cautious, potentially delisting smaller vendors or making it harder for them to operate, thus stifling local businesses and reducing consumer choice.

Critics also contend that the existing regulatory framework, if properly enforced, is adequate. The FSSAI already has provisions for fines ranging from ₹25,000 to ₹10 lakh and even imprisonment for severe violations, alongside consumer protection laws allowing for refunds and compensation. The focus, they argue, should be on strengthening enforcement mechanisms, ensuring consistent inspections, and addressing existing challenges like liability sharing and delivery personnel training, rather than simply increasing the fine amounts.

Key facts

  • FSSAI intensified quick-commerce inspections from September 2, 2026, after consumer complaints of expired and contaminated food in July-August 2026.
  • Selling unsafe or expired food is a criminal offense under FSSA 2006, punishable by up to 6 months imprisonment and a ₹5 lakh fine.
  • Recent inspections by state food authorities led to license suspensions for several quick-commerce dark stores due to hygiene issues and expired stock.

The bottom line

The debate over higher fines for quick-commerce platforms highlights a critical tension between consumer safety and business operational realities. While the urgency to protect consumers from unsafe food is clear, the implications of increased penalties on the industry's ecosystem, including vendors and pricing, also warrant careful consideration. The path forward likely involves a multi-faceted approach that balances robust regulation with sustainable business practices to ensure both rapid delivery and assured food safety.
