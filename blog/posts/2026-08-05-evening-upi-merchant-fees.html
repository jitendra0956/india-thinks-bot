Decoding UPI: Should Merchants Pay Transaction Fees?

August 05, 2026 · 3 min read

India's Unified Payments Interface (UPI) has revolutionized digital transactions, making them seamless and accessible. However, a significant policy debate is resurfacing regarding the sustainability of its 'free for merchants' model. On August 4, 2026, proposed changes to payment laws were introduced in parliament, moving India closer to potentially reintroducing merchant fees on select UPI transactions. This development brings into focus the delicate balance between fostering digital adoption and ensuring the financial viability of the payment ecosystem.

Why people say yes

Proponents argue that introducing merchant fees is crucial for the long-term sustainability and growth of the UPI ecosystem. Payment service providers and banks incur significant costs for developing, maintaining, and upgrading the robust infrastructure required for UPI's smooth functioning, including security and fraud prevention measures. Currently, these costs are largely absorbed by the institutions, with government subsidies only partially covering the actual expenses. Industry stakeholders believe that a steady revenue stream from transaction fees, known as Merchant Discount Rate (MDR), is essential to encourage continued investment and innovation, preventing the rapid growth of UPI from plateauing.

Furthermore, advocates suggest that it's a matter of fair cost sharing for the value provided by digital payment services. They point out that credit and debit card transactions already involve MDRs for merchants, typically ranging from 0.9% to 1.5%. The proposed fees for UPI are considerably lower, often between 0.25% and 0.5%, and are primarily targeted at high-value transactions (e.g., above ₹2,000) and larger merchants. This tiered approach aims to ensure that small businesses and everyday low-value transactions, which constitute the majority of UPI's volume, remain unaffected, thereby protecting financial inclusion.

Why people say no

Opponents express concerns that reintroducing merchant fees could burden businesses, potentially eroding their profit margins. Small and medium enterprises, while potentially exempt from some proposed charges, might still face increased operational costs if the fee structure expands or if larger merchants pass on these costs. This could lead to a less favorable environment for digital payments, especially for businesses that have benefited significantly from UPI's zero-cost model since its inception.

Another significant worry is the potential impact on UPI's phenomenal adoption rate. The system's widespread success in India is partly attributed to its user-friendly interface and the absence of transaction costs for merchants. Introducing fees, even if targeted, could deter some merchants from fully embracing UPI or encourage them to favor other payment methods, potentially slowing down the nation's push towards a less-cash economy. Policymakers are navigating a delicate political landscape, aiming to balance funding needs without hindering UPI's momentum.

Critics also fear that the burden of these fees could ultimately be passed on to consumers through higher prices for goods and services. While technically an MDR is paid by the merchant, businesses often factor such operational costs into their pricing strategies. Although the current proposals aim to keep consumer-to-consumer (P2P) and small merchant transactions free, any shift in cost for larger transactions might indirectly affect the end consumer, undermining the 'frictionless cost' appeal of UPI.

Key facts

  • India's Parliament introduced amendments to the Payment and Settlement Systems Act on August 4, 2026, to create a legal basis for charging Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) on digital payments.
  • Proposed MDRs are expected to apply to transactions above ₹2,000 and/or to larger merchants with annual turnovers exceeding certain thresholds, while small businesses and consumers remain exempt.
  • In July 2026, UPI processed approximately 23.7 billion transactions, valuing nearly ₹29.9 trillion (or ₹29.9 lakh crore), demonstrating its massive scale.

The bottom line

The debate over reintroducing merchant fees on UPI transactions highlights a crucial juncture in India's digital payment journey. On one side lies the imperative to ensure the financial health and long-term innovation of the UPI system, allowing payment providers to cover their operational costs and invest in future advancements. On the other side is the concern for business burden, potential impacts on digital adoption, and the possibility of increased costs for consumers. The proposed legislative changes suggest a nuanced approach, aiming to strike a balance by targeting specific transaction values and merchant sizes, ensuring that UPI continues to thrive while finding a sustainable funding model. The final decisions on the fee structure and its implementation will be pivotal in shaping the future of digital payments in India.
