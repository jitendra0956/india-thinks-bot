Decoding UPI Charges: The Debate Over Fees on Larger Merchant Transactions

September 17, 2026 · 3 min read

India's digital payment landscape is buzzing with a new debate following the Finance Ministry's notification on September 15, 2026. The rules clarified that banks and system providers cannot impose charges on UPI transactions up to Rs 2,000 and RuPay debit card payments, effectively paving the way for potential charges on larger merchant transactions. This move marks a significant shift from the long-standing zero-MDR regime, sparking discussions across the nation about its implications for businesses, consumers, and the future of digital payments. The central question now is: should UPI payments above Rs 2,000 incur merchant charges?

Why people say yes

Proponents argue that introducing a Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) on larger UPI transactions is crucial for the long-term sustainability and growth of the UPI ecosystem. For years, UPI operated with a zero-MDR policy, with the government providing incentives to cover the operational costs for banks and payment service providers. However, with the exponential growth in transaction volumes, the existing funding model has become financially unsustainable. The revenue generated from MDR can be reinvested into developing robust infrastructure, enhancing cybersecurity measures, preventing fraud, and fostering innovation within the UPI network.

Furthermore, charging MDR on higher-value merchant transactions helps level the playing field with other digital payment methods. Traditional credit and debit card transactions already incur MDRs, which merchants routinely absorb or factor into their pricing. By introducing a comparable fee for larger UPI transactions, the argument is that UPI can become a self-sustaining system, reducing its reliance on government subsidies and promoting healthy competition and investment among payment providers. This approach ensures that the costs associated with running a secure and efficient payment network are shared more equitably among the ecosystem participants who benefit from it.

Another point is that the MDR is designed to primarily impact person-to-merchant (P2M) transactions above a certain threshold, specifically Rs 2,000. This means that day-to-day, low-value transactions, which constitute the vast majority of UPI's volume, will remain free for both consumers and many small merchants. The focus on higher-value transactions aims to recover costs from commercial activities that often involve significant value transfers, rather than burdening ordinary users or micro-enterprises.

Why people say no

Opponents, including various merchant associations and political parties, express strong reservations, primarily fearing the negative impact on small businesses and the broader digital adoption drive. The Retailers Association of India (RAI), for instance, has warned that imposing a 0.4% MDR on transactions above Rs 2,000 could reverse the significant gains in digital payment adoption and push small businesses back to cash, especially as many operate on thin margins. Even though the government states the charge is for merchants, the concern is that these businesses will ultimately pass on the costs to consumers through higher prices for goods and services.

Critics also highlight that the introduction of charges could undermine the fundamental principle of UPI as a free, public digital infrastructure that has democratized digital payments across India. They argue that making any part of UPI chargeable, even for merchants, contradicts the vision of a universally accessible and cost-free payment system that has been instrumental in formalizing the economy and boosting GST reporting. The psychological effect of even a small fee could deter merchants from accepting UPI for larger transactions, potentially stalling the momentum of digital payment growth.

Furthermore, there are concerns about the timing and implementation of these charges. Opposition parties have questioned the transparency of the decision-making process, with some alleging external pressure. While the government has clarified that consumers will not be directly charged and has advised banks to prevent merchants from passing on the MDR, the practical reality for small businesses might be different. The burden of absorbing these costs or navigating complex exemptions could be challenging for many, potentially leading to a fragmented payment experience or reduced enthusiasm for UPI acceptance.

Key facts

  • The Finance Ministry notified rules on September 15, 2026, clarifying that banks and system providers cannot impose charges on UPI transactions up to Rs 2,000, implicitly setting the stage for potential charges on larger merchant transactions.
  • A 0.4% Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) will be levied on Person-to-Merchant (P2M) UPI transactions exceeding Rs 2,000, effective October 15, 2026, to be borne by merchants.
  • Person-to-person (P2P) UPI transactions, person-to-merchant (P2M) transactions up to Rs 2,000, and payments to small merchants receiving up to Rs 1 lakh per month via UPI QR codes will continue to remain free of charge.

The bottom line

The introduction of MDR on higher-value UPI merchant transactions represents a pivotal moment for India's digital payments. While it aims to ensure the financial viability and continued innovation of the UPI ecosystem, it also raises legitimate concerns about the potential impact on small businesses and consumer costs. The success of this policy will depend on careful monitoring of its effects and the ability of the ecosystem to balance sustainability with inclusivity, ensuring that UPI continues to serve as a powerful engine for digital economic growth.
