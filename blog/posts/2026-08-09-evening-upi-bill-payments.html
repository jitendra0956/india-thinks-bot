UPI Bill: Will India's Digital Payments Remain Free?

August 09, 2026 · 3 min read

The Finance Ministry introduced a significant bill on August 4, 2026, aimed at amending the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007. This proposed legislation empowers the government to determine the Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) for certain UPI transactions in the future. This move has sparked a nationwide debate: will this bill jeopardize the 'free' nature of digital payments in India, a cornerstone of its digital revolution, or is it a necessary step for the ecosystem's long-term health?

Why people say yes

Proponents of this view argue that empowering the government to levy MDR, even if initially nominal and selective, opens the door for increased costs within the UPI ecosystem. While the government clarifies that MDR applies to merchants, critics fear these costs will eventually be passed on to consumers through higher prices for goods and services. This could erode the very affordability that drove UPI's massive adoption among millions of Indians, potentially pushing some users back to cash transactions.

Another concern is the potential impact on innovation and competition. The zero-MDR regime, while challenging for profitability, fostered a landscape where payment service providers focused on value-added services and user acquisition. Introducing MDR, even for specific transaction types, might discourage new fintech players or limit the ability of existing ones to invest in advanced technologies and cybersecurity, as their revenue models become tied to transaction fees. This could slow the rapid pace of digital payment evolution India has witnessed.

Furthermore, some argue that even if small merchants are initially exempt, the policy could evolve over time to include more categories or lower thresholds. This gradual expansion could disproportionately affect small businesses, who rely heavily on free UPI payments for cost-effective transactions. Concerns have also been raised regarding potential external pressures influencing such policy shifts, questioning if the move is partly in response to demands from international payment giants.

Why people say no

Conversely, supporters of the bill emphasize that it is an essential step towards ensuring the long-term sustainability and robustness of India's digital payment infrastructure. The current zero-MDR policy, in place since January 2020, has meant that banks and payment service providers have been absorbing significant operational and infrastructure costs without a direct revenue stream from transactions. The government's subsidies to compensate these entities have been inconsistent and often insufficient to cover the actual costs of maintaining and expanding the vast UPI network.

The government has explicitly clarified that the bill is an *enabling provision* and does not immediately impose charges. Crucially, it has assured that UPI transactions will remain completely free for consumers and for all person-to-person (P2P) payments. Any MDR, if introduced, would be nominal, threshold-based (e.g., for merchant transactions above ₹2,000), and significantly lower than charges on credit or debit cards. This approach aims to create a sustainable revenue model to fund continuous investment in cybersecurity, fraud prevention, and expansion into rural and semi-urban areas.

This nuanced approach is intended to strike a balance: protect the common user and small merchant, while allowing payment ecosystem players to recover costs and invest in future growth. By ensuring a sustainable financial model, the bill aims to prevent potential degradation of service quality or stagnation of innovation due to lack of funds. The objective is to secure UPI's future as an inclusive and advanced payment system, not to dismantle its core 'free for users' principle.

Key facts

  • The Finance Ministry introduced The Taxation and Other Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026, on August 4, 2026, to allow the government to decide on MDR for certain UPI transactions.
  • The government has clarified that UPI will remain free for consumers and all person-to-person (P2P) transactions.
  • Any Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) introduced would be nominal, threshold-based (e.g., for merchant transactions above ₹2,000), and apply to a limited set of merchants, not all.

The bottom line

The new UPI bill represents a pivotal moment for India's digital payment landscape. While it grants the government the power to introduce MDR, the stated intent is to ensure the long-term sustainability of the UPI ecosystem while keeping most transactions free for users and small merchants. The debate centers on whether this enabling provision will lead to an erosion of the 'free payments' model or responsibly fund the necessary infrastructure for UPI's continued growth and expansion across India.
