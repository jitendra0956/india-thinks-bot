AI's Shadow: Should India Create a Special Fund for Displaced Tech Workers?

July 26, 2026 · 3 min read

Reports emerging between July 9-19, 2026, highlight a significant shift in India's IT sector, with projections indicating that up to 35,000 tech jobs could be eliminated this year due to the rapid adoption of AI and other productivity enhancements. This news has ignited a critical debate: should the government establish a dedicated fund to support tech workers who find themselves displaced by this technological revolution? The question forces a national conversation about economic responsibility, worker welfare, and the future of India's robust tech industry.

Why people say yes

Proponents argue that a special government fund is crucial for providing a much-needed safety net for tech professionals facing unexpected job losses. Unlike many Western nations, India lacks comprehensive unemployment benefits or robust retirement safety nets, leaving displaced workers vulnerable. Furthermore, even severance pay in India is subject to taxation, diminishing the financial cushion available during periods of job uncertainty. A dedicated fund could offer direct financial assistance, helping individuals and families navigate this challenging transition.

Such a fund could also play a pivotal role in facilitating the reskilling and upskilling of displaced workers. As AI redefines job roles, many existing skills become redundant, while new, high-demand proficiencies emerge. Financial support from the government could enable workers, particularly those in middle and lower-wage brackets most susceptible to automation, to enroll in specialized training programs and acquire the skills necessary for the evolving job market. This investment in human capital could prevent long-term unemployment and foster a smoother transition into new roles.

Finally, establishing a special fund could address concerns about economic inequality and corporate accountability. Critics suggest that AI adoption disproportionately benefits corporate shareholders through increased profits and efficiency, while workers bear the brunt of job displacement. A government fund, potentially financed through levies or taxes on AI-related corporate profits, could redistribute some of this AI-generated wealth to the broader population. This would ensure that the benefits of technological advancement are shared more equitably, reflecting a commitment to inclusive growth and worker welfare during a period of significant economic transformation.

Why people say no

Opponents of a special fund contend that the Indian government already has numerous initiatives in place to address skill development and employment. Programs like the Pradhan Mantri Kaushal Vikas Yojana (PMKVY 4.0), FutureSkills Prime, and the FutureSkills Pillar under the IndiaAI Mission are actively working to upskill and reskill the workforce in emerging technologies. These existing frameworks aim to build a strong AI talent ecosystem and prepare individuals for future-ready roles, making a new, separate fund potentially redundant.

Furthermore, the argument is made that while AI displaces some jobs, it simultaneously creates many new opportunities, particularly in specialized AI-related fields. In June 2026, AI hiring in India's IT sector surged by 16% year-on-year, even as overall IT job listings saw a 3% decline. Projections indicate a 32% increase in AI-related job postings in 2026. The focus, therefore, should remain on empowering individuals to adapt and acquire these new skills rather than providing direct financial aid for displacement, which might inadvertently disincentivize proactive reskilling.

Lastly, concerns are raised about the potential financial burden on taxpayers and the risk of fostering dependency. Creating a new, extensive government fund would require substantial public resources, which could either lead to increased taxes or divert funds from other critical sectors. Some argue that the market, driven by its inherent adaptive mechanisms, will eventually absorb displaced workers into new roles as companies continue to innovate. Over-reliance on government funds could stifle this natural market adaptation and potentially create a precedent for intervention in every industry facing technological disruption.

Key facts

  • Estimates indicate that India's IT sector could eliminate between 25,000 and 35,000 jobs in 2026 due to AI and productivity improvements.
  • AI-specific hiring in India's IT sector rose 16% year-on-year in June 2026, while overall IT job listings declined by 3%.
  • Only approximately 16% of existing IT professionals in India currently possess AI-ready skills.

The bottom line

The debate over a special fund for tech workers displaced by AI in India highlights the complex challenges and opportunities presented by rapid technological advancement. While the human cost of job displacement is a significant concern, the ongoing efforts in skill development and the potential for new job creation also offer a path forward. Ultimately, the nation must weigh the immediate needs of affected individuals against broader economic strategies for fostering a future-ready and resilient workforce.
