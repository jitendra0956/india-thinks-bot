Online Battleground: Are Social Media Platforms Doing Enough to Curb Illegal Content?

September 03, 2026 · 3 min read

The digital world, while connecting billions, presents a constant challenge: the proliferation of illegal content. This issue was starkly highlighted on September 3, 2026, when the National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) took cognisance of reports alleging that paid advertisements on Meta platforms were promoting child sexual abuse material in India. The incident has intensified the debate: are social media platforms truly doing enough to combat illegal content, or are their efforts falling short?

Why people say yes

Proponents argue that social media platforms are making significant strides in content moderation. Companies invest heavily in advanced Artificial Intelligence (AI) and machine learning tools, which are crucial for detecting and removing harmful content at an unprecedented scale. These AI systems can identify patterns, keywords, and images associated with illegal material, acting as a first line of defence. Furthermore, platforms employ large teams of human moderators who provide the necessary nuanced review that AI often misses, especially concerning context and cultural intricacies. These hybrid moderation models are constantly evolving, with platforms regularly updating their community guidelines and safety technologies to address emerging threats and comply with stringent regulations like India's IT Rules, 2021, and the UK's Online Safety Act.

Platforms often highlight their proactive efforts, claiming to remove millions of accounts and pieces of content even before they are reported by users. For instance, Meta stated it removed 160,000 accounts in India related to child exploitation in six months and over four million globally in a year for suspicious activity. They also point to reporting mechanisms and user education as integral parts of their strategy, empowering the community to flag problematic content. While acknowledging challenges, the sheer volume of content successfully moderated demonstrates a substantial commitment to online safety, making the internet a safer place than it would be without these robust systems.

Why people say no

Critics, however, contend that despite these efforts, platforms are not doing enough, with illegal content persistently slipping through the cracks. The sheer volume of user-generated content, with billions of items uploaded daily across diverse languages and cultural contexts, overwhelms even the most sophisticated moderation systems. Even a high accuracy rate by AI can still result in millions of missed harmful items daily. The NHRC's recent cognisance regarding Meta underscores this failure, revealing that alleged paid advertisements promoting child sexual abuse material passed through Meta's review mechanisms and remained accessible until specifically flagged by external reports.

Moreover, AI struggles significantly with detecting nuanced forms of illegal content, such as 'algospeak' – coded language used to bypass moderation filters – or content that requires deep contextual understanding. This allows malicious actors to adapt quickly, finding new ways to exploit platform loopholes. The profit-driven business models of some platforms are also scrutinised, with accusations that rapid growth and engagement are prioritised over stringent safety measures. Furthermore, delays in content takedown, even for clearly illegal material, render moderation ineffective as harmful content can spread virally before removal. While some platforms like Meta have introduced new child safety measures, critics note these are often reactive and, in cases like India, not yet fully implemented or tailored to local conditions.

Key facts

  • On September 3, 2026, the NHRC took cognisance of reports claiming paid ads on Meta platforms promoted child sexual abuse material in India.
  • Social media platforms manage billions of content items daily across numerous languages, posing immense moderation challenges.
  • AI content moderation is widely used, but human judgment remains essential for context-dependent and nuanced content decisions.

The bottom line

The debate over social media platforms' effectiveness in curbing illegal content is complex, balancing technological advancements against the overwhelming scale and evolving nature of harmful material. While platforms have invested heavily in AI, human moderation, and policy frameworks, incidents like the NHRC's recent action on Meta highlight persistent gaps. The challenge lies in developing more robust, context-aware systems that can keep pace with malicious actors, ensure proactive enforcement, and adapt to diverse global contexts, without stifling legitimate expression. Ultimately, the question remains whether current measures are truly sufficient to protect users from the most egregious forms of online harm.
