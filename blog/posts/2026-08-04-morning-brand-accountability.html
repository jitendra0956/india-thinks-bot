Product Safety Under Scrutiny: How Accountable Are Brands?

August 04, 2026 · 3 min read

A recent incident in Pune, where a consumer allegedly found flesh-like objects inside a sealed Samrat Rava pack, has once again brought the critical issue of product contamination and brand accountability to the forefront. This complaint, filed with the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) on August 2, 2026, sparks a vital debate: are current regulations sufficient, or should brands face stricter penalties and oversight for ensuring the safety and purity of their products? The incident underscores the inherent trust consumers place in packaged goods and the severe implications when that trust is broken due to contamination.

Why people say yes

Advocates for stricter brand accountability argue that enhanced regulations are crucial for safeguarding public health. When brands are held to higher standards, it forces them to implement more rigorous quality control measures throughout their supply chains, from sourcing raw materials to final packaging. This proactive approach can significantly reduce the incidence of contamination, preventing potential illnesses or even fatalities among consumers.

Increased accountability can also foster greater transparency within the food industry. If brands know they face severe repercussions for lapses, they are more likely to invest in advanced traceability systems and openly communicate any issues. This transparency builds greater consumer trust, which is essential for a healthy market where consumers feel confident in their purchases.

Furthermore, stricter accountability acts as a powerful deterrent. Current penalties, while significant, may not always be perceived as sufficient to outweigh the potential cost-saving measures that could lead to negligence. Higher fines, more severe legal consequences, and mandatory public disclosures can compel brands to prioritize food safety not just as a compliance issue, but as a fundamental ethical imperative.

Why people say no

Conversely, opponents of increased brand accountability argue that existing legal frameworks in India are already robust enough. The Food Safety and Standards Act (FSSA), 2006, along with the Consumer Protection Act (CPA), 2019, outline clear responsibilities for manufacturers, sellers, and service providers, including provisions for penalties and compensation for defective or unsafe products. These acts provide a comprehensive structure for addressing consumer grievances and punishing non-compliance.

Another concern is the potential for increased compliance costs to be passed on to consumers. Implementing even stricter quality control measures, advanced testing, and enhanced traceability systems can be expensive, particularly for smaller and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs). These additional costs could lead to higher product prices, disproportionately affecting lower-income households and potentially making essential goods less affordable.

Moreover, some argue that overly stringent regulations could create an environment ripe for false claims or misuse of the system. While consumer protection is vital, a balance must be struck to prevent frivolous complaints that could unfairly damage a brand's reputation and incur unnecessary legal and operational costs, especially in cases where contamination might occur beyond the brand's direct control in a complex supply chain.

Key facts

  • The Food Safety and Standards Act (FSSA), 2006, and its regulations empower the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) to set standards and enforce rules for food manufacturing, processing, storage, and sale.
  • Under the FSSA, penalties for selling substandard, misbranded, or unsafe food can range from fines up to ₹10 lakh and, in severe cases involving grievous injury or death, include imprisonment for up to life.
  • The Consumer Protection Act (CPA), 2019, introduced a comprehensive product liability framework, holding manufacturers, sellers, and service providers responsible for harm caused by defective products, shifting the focus from 'buyer beware' to 'seller beware'.

The bottom line

The debate over brand accountability for product contamination highlights a fundamental tension between consumer safety and business operational realities. While the Pune incident underscores the critical need for safe food products, the path to achieving this involves navigating complex legal frameworks, economic implications, and the intricacies of supply chains. Ensuring that consumers are protected while fostering a responsible and sustainable industry remains a shared challenge that requires ongoing dialogue and adaptive regulatory approaches from all stakeholders.
