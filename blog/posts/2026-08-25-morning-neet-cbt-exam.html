NEET-UG: The Debate Over Shifting to Computer-Based Testing

August 25, 2026 · 3 min read

The Centre's recent disclosure to the Supreme Court on August 5, 2026, indicating that the shift of the NEET-UG exam to a computer-based testing (CBT) format is under active consideration by the Nilekani committee, has sparked a significant debate. This potential move, aimed at reforming India's most competitive medical entrance examination, comes amidst ongoing scrutiny over exam security and fairness. With millions of aspirants competing annually for limited medical seats, any change to the examination format holds profound implications for students, educators, and the future of medical education in the country.

Why people say yes

Proponents argue that a full transition to computer-based testing would significantly enhance exam security and integrity. The current pen-and-paper format has been vulnerable to issues like question paper leaks, organized cheating, and impersonation. CBT can drastically reduce these risks by enabling encrypted test papers to be transmitted digitally just minutes before the exam, eliminating the physical handling and transportation of papers. This digital approach offers centralized monitoring and controlled access environments, making malpractices harder to execute.

Beyond security, CBT promises greater efficiency and accuracy in the examination process. Automated assessment and digital workflows streamline administration, accelerating result processing from weeks to days. This not only benefits candidates by providing quicker outcomes but also reduces manual effort for examination authorities. Furthermore, CBT can provide a more standardized examination experience across various centers through consistent delivery, secure access, and uniform interfaces.

The scalability of computer-based platforms is another key advantage, especially for an exam like NEET-UG that accommodates over 2 million candidates annually. Modern CBT platforms, often cloud-based, can support thousands of candidates simultaneously across multiple centers. This infrastructure can also simplify logistics, such as center allocation and session management, reducing operational errors and facilitating rapid expansion across regions without increasing operational risk.

Why people say no

Despite the benefits, critics raise significant concerns about the readiness of India's diverse student population and infrastructure for a complete shift to CBT. The most prominent issue is the 'digital divide,' particularly affecting students from rural and economically disadvantaged backgrounds. Data indicates a stark inequality in computer ownership, with only 4.7% of rural households owning a computer or laptop compared to 14.9% in urban areas. This disparity means many rural students lack basic computer literacy and familiarity with digital interfaces, potentially putting them at an unfair disadvantage regardless of their academic merit.

Technical glitches and infrastructure limitations present another major hurdle. Concerns include potential hardware malfunctions, unreliable internet connectivity, and frequent power outages in many rural regions. Even minor technical issues during an exam can cause significant stress and disrupt a candidate's performance, leading to an inconsistent and unfair testing environment. Ensuring 24/7 technical and operational support across thousands of centers would be a monumental challenge.

Furthermore, if NEET-UG were to be conducted in multiple shifts due to the sheer volume of candidates and limited testing centers, maintaining absolute parity in difficulty across sessions becomes challenging. This could necessitate a normalization model, similar to JEE Main, where scores are adjusted based on session difficulty, potentially leading to varied percentiles for the same raw score and creating anxiety among students. The transition also implies significant costs for coaching institutes to upgrade their infrastructure and for students from financially constrained families to access computers or practice tests.

Key facts

  • The Centre informed the Supreme Court on August 5, 2026, that shifting NEET-UG to computer-based testing is under active consideration by the Nilekani committee.
  • Computer-based testing can significantly reduce the risks of physical paper leaks, large-scale copying, organized cheating, and impersonation in large-scale exams.
  • Only 4.7% of rural households in India own a computer or laptop, compared with 14.9% of urban households, highlighting a significant digital divide.

The bottom line

The debate over converting NEET-UG to a computer-based format underscores the complex balance between modernizing examination practices for security and efficiency, and ensuring equitable access and opportunity for all students across India. While the move could address long-standing issues of paper leaks and administrative challenges, careful consideration must be given to bridging the digital divide, upgrading infrastructure, and providing adequate training to ensure a fair and inclusive transition for every medical aspirant. The recommendations of the Nilekani committee and stakeholder consultations will be crucial in shaping the future of this pivotal exam.
