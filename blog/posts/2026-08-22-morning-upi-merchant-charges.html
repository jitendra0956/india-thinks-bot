The UPI Crossroads: Balancing Innovation, Inclusion, and Sustainability with Merchant Charges

August 22, 2026 · 3 min read

India's Unified Payments Interface (UPI) has been a global success story, transforming digital payments with its ease and cost-effectiveness. However, a recent legislative change — the passing of the Taxation and Other Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026, which received presidential assent on August 17, 2026 — has opened the door to charging for UPI merchant transactions above a certain threshold. This development has ignited a crucial debate: how to ensure the long-term sustainability and continued innovation of UPI without hindering its widespread adoption and the financial inclusion it has fostered.

Why people say yes

Proponents argue that introducing charges on higher-value UPI merchant transactions is essential for the ecosystem's sustainability. The infrastructure and security required to handle billions of transactions annually incur significant costs for banks, payment service providers, and NPCI. Currently, these costs are largely absorbed by the government through incentives or by banks. A Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) would provide a sustainable revenue model, encouraging private players to invest further in technology, cybersecurity, fraud prevention, and system upgrades. This would wean the ecosystem off a subsidy model, fostering greater competition and resilience, much like other payment systems globally. RBI Governor Sanjay Malhotra has also emphasized that the cost of maintaining UPI's secure and reliable infrastructure must be borne by someone.

Why people say no

Opponents fear that introducing charges, even on a limited set of merchant transactions, could jeopardize UPI's phenomenal success in driving digital adoption and financial inclusion. They argue that even a nominal fee could deter small and medium enterprises (SMEs) that operate on thin margins, potentially driving them back to cash transactions. This would negate the benefits of reduced cash handling and increased transparency that UPI has brought to the economy. Furthermore, some argue that banks have actually seen reduced operational costs due to UPI and have not historically demanded MDR. The argument is also made that UPI, as a public good, should remain free, with its costs potentially funded from the savings it generates for the broader economy and the government, such as reduced currency printing costs. There are also concerns that merchants might pass on these charges to consumers, ultimately making digital payments less attractive.

Key facts

  • The Taxation and Other Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026, made it legally possible to charge for UPI, though the government has stated consumers and small merchants would remain protected.
  • UPI processed 23.6 billion transactions worth approximately $313.5 billion in July 2026 alone, making it one of the world's largest real-time payment networks.
  • Proposals under discussion suggest an MDR of 0.25% to 0.5% on higher-value transactions (e.g., above ₹2,000 or for large merchants), which account for a significant portion of transaction value but a small percentage of volume.

The bottom line

The debate over charging for UPI merchant transactions highlights the complex challenge of sustaining a world-leading digital public infrastructure. While the need for a viable revenue model to fund ongoing innovation and operational costs is clear, policymakers must carefully balance this with the imperative to maintain UPI's accessibility and affordability, especially for small businesses and everyday users. The ultimate solution will likely involve a nuanced approach that ensures financial sustainability without undermining the foundational principles of inclusion and convenience that have made UPI such a transformative force in India's economy.
