Student Protests: Balancing Order and Rights

July 27, 2026 · 3 min read

The recent student protests at Jantar Mantar on July 27, 2026, over the NEET paper leak, reportedly saw an RAF personnel firing pellet rounds. This incident, following a series of similar allegations of police action during student demonstrations, has reignited a crucial debate: how should law enforcement manage public dissent while upholding constitutional rights? The question of whether police tactics in student protests require more regulation is at the forefront of national discourse, emphasizing the delicate balance between maintaining public order and protecting the fundamental right to protest.

Why people say yes

Calls for greater regulation often stem from concerns about the indiscriminate nature of certain crowd control measures and the potential for excessive force. Advocates argue that tactics like pellet guns, despite being classified as "less-lethal," have caused grievous and sometimes permanent injuries, including loss of eyesight, as documented in past protests. Stricter regulations, they contend, would mandate a graded response, ensuring that force is used only as a last resort and is proportionate to the threat, aligning with international human rights standards on necessity and proportionality. Furthermore, proponents believe that clear national guidelines would prevent arbitrary actions and promote accountability among law enforcement personnel, many of whom have been accused of using force disproportionately or without proper identification. The fragmented legal framework, with varying state-level rules and executive orders, necessitates a uniform, comprehensive statutory framework to govern police actions during demonstrations.

Ensuring the protection of fundamental rights, particularly the right to peaceful assembly and freedom of speech, is another key argument for increased regulation. When protests are met with heavy-handed responses, it can stifle legitimate dissent and erode public trust in democratic institutions. Regulations could enforce transparent procedures, such as mandatory warnings before using coercive measures and independent investigations into allegations of police brutality. This would safeguard citizens' ability to voice grievances without fear of undue harm or arbitrary detention, reinforcing the constitutional promise of a participatory democracy.

Finally, standardizing protocols across the country could lead to more professional and predictable police responses. Instead of relying on ad-hoc decisions, a regulated approach would equip police with better training, appropriate non-lethal alternatives, and clear escalation matrices, reducing the likelihood of chaotic and injurious clashes. This would not only protect protesters but also provide clarity and safety for police personnel navigating challenging crowd control situations. The Supreme Court itself has underscored the need for uniform guidelines governing police response to public demonstrations across the country.

Why people say no

Conversely, those against further regulation argue that existing laws and guidelines already provide a framework for managing protests, emphasizing the need for police to maintain public order and security. They contend that the right to protest is not absolute and is subject to reasonable restrictions in the interest of public order. Police often face rapidly evolving and potentially volatile situations where immediate action is necessary to prevent widespread disruption, damage to property, or escalation to violence. Imposing overly rigid regulations, it is argued, could hinder police effectiveness in these dynamic environments, putting both the public and law enforcement personnel at risk.

Furthermore, proponents of the current approach highlight that police require a degree of flexibility to respond effectively to different types of protests and crowd behaviors. What might be appropriate force in one scenario could be insufficient or excessive in another. The legal framework, including sections of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS) and the Code of Criminal Procedure (CrPC), already empowers police to disperse unlawful assemblies using civil or, if necessary, armed force, with the caveat of using minimal injury. Additional layers of regulation, critics suggest, might create bureaucratic hurdles that delay necessary interventions, allowing situations to spiral out of control.

Lastly, some argue that the focus should be on stricter adherence to existing guidelines and better training, rather than introducing new regulations. Police manuals and codes of conduct already advocate for persuasion and minimal force. The challenge, they believe, lies in implementation and accountability for individual actions, rather than a lack of regulatory framework. Allegations of police brutality, as seen in recent protests, may stem from operational failures or individual misconduct, rather than systemic gaps in the legal provisions. The Supreme Court has also acknowledged that injuries suffered by police personnel deserve equal concern.

Key facts

  • Article 19(1)(b) of the Indian Constitution guarantees citizens the right to assemble peaceably and without arms, which forms the basis of the right to protest.
  • Indian law mandates that police must use the "irreducible minimum force" when dispersing unlawful assemblies, with international standards emphasizing necessity and proportionality.
  • The use of pellet guns for crowd control has been criticized by human rights organizations due to the severe injuries, including permanent blindness, they can cause.

The bottom line

The debate over regulating police tactics in student protests in India reflects a fundamental tension between the right to express dissent and the state's responsibility to maintain public order. While incidents of alleged excessive force during recent student demonstrations have fueled calls for stricter, more standardized protocols to protect protesters' rights and prevent injuries, others argue that existing laws provide sufficient guidance and that police need operational flexibility to manage unpredictable crowd situations. A path forward likely involves a careful re-evaluation of current practices, enhanced training, and robust accountability mechanisms, all within the constitutional framework that champions both individual liberties and collective security.
