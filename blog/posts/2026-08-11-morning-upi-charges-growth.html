Decoding UPI Charges: A Path to Growth or a Roadblock for Digital India?

August 11, 2026 · 3 min read

The Indian Parliament recently passed the Taxation and Other Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026, a move that has ignited discussions across the nation. This new legislation empowers the government to potentially introduce Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) on certain digital payment transactions, particularly through the widely popular Unified Payments Interface (UPI). While the Ministry of Finance has assured that consumers will not bear any direct charges for UPI transactions, and most small merchant payments will remain free, the debate centers on whether such charges, even if limited, will foster the long-term sustainability and growth of India's digital economy or hinder its remarkable progress.

Why people say yes

Ensuring UPI's Sustainability: Proponents argue that introducing MDR on select merchant transactions is crucial for the long-term sustainability of the UPI ecosystem. Operating and constantly upgrading the digital payment infrastructure, including cybersecurity and fraud prevention, incurs significant costs that cannot be perpetually borne by subsidies alone. A revenue model through MDR would provide the necessary funds for these ongoing investments. The RBI Governor Sanjay Malhotra has also emphasized that the cost of providing payment services must ultimately be paid by someone.

Attracting Investment and Innovation: A viable revenue stream for banks and fintech companies could incentivize greater private investment and foster innovation within the digital payments sector. Currently, the zero-MDR regime, while promoting adoption, limits the ability of these entities to recover costs and invest in new technologies and expanded services, especially in rural and semi-urban areas. MDR could unlock a sustainable business model, attracting more players and driving competition.

Reducing Reliance on Government Subsidies: The existing zero-MDR policy has largely been supported by government subsidies to compensate the industry. However, this model is deemed unsustainable in the long run given the exponential growth of UPI transactions. Shifting towards a partial MDR model for high-value merchant transactions could reduce the financial burden on the government and create a more self-reliant digital payment ecosystem. This ensures that the system can scale without constant public funding.

Why people say no

Potential for Increased Costs for Businesses: Critics express concern that even a nominal MDR, if applied to merchants, could increase their operational costs. While the government has stated that small merchants will be exempt and charges will be threshold-based, businesses, particularly Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs) handling a large volume of digital transactions, fear an additional financial burden. This could erode thin profit margins for some.

Risk of Slowing Digital Adoption: A key success factor for UPI has been its zero-cost nature, which significantly accelerated digital payment adoption across India, including among small vendors and consumers in informal sectors. Introducing any charges, even if indirect or limited, carries the risk of discouraging further digital adoption or even pushing some merchants and users back towards cash transactions, thereby reversing gains in financial inclusion and formalization.

Small businesses fear financial burden: Although the government has clarified that consumers will not directly pay UPI charges, there is apprehension that merchants might eventually pass on the MDR cost to consumers by increasing prices of goods and services. This indirect burden could affect household budgets and undermine the perceived "free" benefit of UPI for the end-user. Ensuring transparency on how these costs are managed will be crucial.

Key facts

  • The Lok Sabha passed the Taxation and Other Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026, around August 6, 2026, enabling the government to permit Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) on digital payments.
  • The Ministry of Finance has assured that consumers and person-to-person (P2P) UPI transactions will remain free, with any potential MDR applying only to a limited set of merchant transactions above a specified threshold and at a nominal rate.
  • The annual cost of running UPI and RuPay debit card infrastructure is estimated to be as high as ₹20,000 crore, while government digital payment incentives only covered about 11% of the industry's costs between 2021-22 and 2024-25.

The bottom line

The debate surrounding future UPI charges highlights a critical juncture for India's digital payments journey. While the government aims to ensure the long-term sustainability and technological advancement of UPI by creating a viable revenue model for payment providers, concerns remain about the potential impact on businesses, particularly small merchants, and the overall pace of digital adoption. The eventual policy framework, including the specific thresholds and rates for MDR, will determine the balance between funding innovation and maintaining the inclusive, low-cost nature that has made UPI a global success story. The nation watches to see if this enabling legislation will truly pave the way for a more robust and self-sustaining digital economy.
