Cultural Moves in Commercial Aisles: The Costco Dance Debate

August 31, 2026 · 3 min read

A recent viral video, circulating on August 31, 2026, captured an Indian woman performing a traditional Thiruvathira dance in a Costco store. This spontaneous act quickly ignited a fervent online debate, with viewers sharply divided over whether such cultural expressions are acceptable in private commercial spaces. The incident has pushed conversations about civic sense, cultural pride, and the evolving nature of public and private spheres into the spotlight.

Why people say yes

Many argue that such cultural expressions infuse joy and vibrancy into otherwise mundane commercial environments. Supporters believe that as long as the performance is non-disruptive and doesn't endanger others, it's a harmless way to share one's heritage and connect with a broader audience. In a diverse country like India, public displays of culture can foster understanding and appreciation, turning a simple shopping trip into a unique, memorable experience. This aligns with the growing trend of "retailtainment," where stores are actively seeking to offer immersive and engaging experiences beyond mere transactions to attract and retain customers.

Furthermore, some contend that limiting cultural expression to designated venues stifles spontaneity and creativity. They argue that traditional dances like Thiruvathira, which is a significant folk dance from Kerala often performed by women for marital bliss or to honor Lord Shiva, deserve visibility in everyday life, helping to keep traditions alive and relevant. If other public acts or even social media stunts are tolerated, why should a cultural dance be singled out for criticism?.

For many, these acts are a form of cultural pride. Showcasing one's heritage, especially when living abroad, can be a powerful affirmation of identity. When done respectfully, it can educate and entertain, creating unexpected moments of connection in anonymous commercial settings. Proponents suggest that a little impromptu dance can brighten someone's day and contribute positively to the store's ambiance, reflecting a more inclusive and less rigid society.

Why people say no

Conversely, a strong contingent argues that private commercial spaces like retail stores are primarily for business transactions and not for personal performances. They emphasize that stores have specific codes of conduct and policies designed to ensure a safe, orderly, and efficient shopping experience for all customers. Unsolicited performances can disrupt the flow of traffic, create noise, block aisles, and potentially pose safety hazards for both the performer and other shoppers.

Critics also highlight the issue of private property rights. While stores are open to the public, they remain private entities with the right to dictate acceptable behavior on their premises. Owners and management have a responsibility to maintain an environment conducive to commerce and customer comfort, and spontaneous performances can infringe upon this right and objective. There's a concern that allowing such acts could set a precedent, leading to a proliferation of disruptive behaviors driven by the pursuit of viral social media content.

Moreover, the debate often touches on "civic sense" and how individual actions reflect on broader communities. Some commentators expressed embarrassment, suggesting that such behaviors by individuals could project a negative image of the Indian community, particularly in international settings. They stress the importance of respecting local rules and social norms in different environments, reserving cultural performances for appropriate venues and occasions.

Key facts

  • Thiruvathira is a traditional folk dance from Kerala, India, primarily performed by women during festivals like Thiruvathira and Onam.
  • Retail stores typically have internal policies and codes of conduct designed to ensure customer safety, efficient operations, and a pleasant shopping environment.
  • While India has legal frameworks to protect traditional knowledge and cultural expressions as intellectual property, these primarily address ownership and misappropriation, not unrestricted public performance rights.

The bottom line

The viral video of the Thiruvathira dance in Costco encapsulates a broader tension between individual freedom of cultural expression and the expectations of behavior in private commercial spaces. While some see it as a joyful, harmless act that enriches the shopping experience, others view it as a breach of civic etiquette and an infringement on property rights. This incident highlights the ongoing challenge of balancing cultural pride with public decorum in an increasingly interconnected world.
