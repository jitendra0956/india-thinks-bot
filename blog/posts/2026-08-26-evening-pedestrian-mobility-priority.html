Pavement or Purgatory: Should Indian Cities Prioritize Pedestrian Mobility?

August 26, 2026 · 3 min read

A Gurugram woman's recent viral post detailing an 8km walk through traffic chaos has ignited a critical debate: should city infrastructure fundamentally prioritize pedestrian mobility? This incident, highlighting the daily struggles of commuters in rapidly urbanizing Indian cities, brings to the forefront the long-standing imbalance in urban planning, where walkers often find themselves relegated to the margins. The discussion isn't just about convenience; it's about safety, health, economic vitality, and the very fabric of urban life.

Why people say yes

Advocates for pedestrian priority argue that shifting focus to walkers offers a multitude of benefits, transforming cities into more livable and sustainable spaces. Firstly, it significantly improves public health by encouraging physical activity and reducing sedentary lifestyles. Improved footpaths in Chennai, for instance, were estimated to prevent around 340 deaths annually from non-communicable diseases. Secondly, prioritizing pedestrians can alleviate severe traffic congestion and reduce environmental pollution. When safe and accessible walking options exist, more people opt for active transport, leading to fewer vehicles on the road, lower CO2 emissions, and better air quality. Chennai's footpath improvements alone are estimated to prevent 4,200 to 12,000 tonnes of CO2-equivalent greenhouse gas emissions annually. Thirdly, walkable cities foster economic growth. Pedestrian-friendly areas attract businesses, boost local commerce through increased foot traffic, and can even raise property values. Studies show a positive correlation between improved walkability and increased local retail spend. Lastly, it enhances safety and social equity. Dedicated pedestrian infrastructure reduces accidents, making streets safer for everyone, especially vulnerable groups like children, the elderly, and persons with disabilities. In Chennai, 95% of survey respondents reported feeling safer on improved streets. It also promotes a stronger sense of community and social interaction.

Why people say no

Conversely, those who argue against an exclusive focus on pedestrian mobility raise concerns about the practicalities and potential drawbacks in the context of India's sprawling cities. A primary concern is the impact on vehicular traffic and the movement of goods. Many Indian cities are already heavily reliant on private vehicles, and a drastic re-prioritization could lead to increased travel times for cars and commercial transport, potentially hindering economic activities that depend on efficient logistics. Gurugram's traffic issues, for example, are often met with proposals for elevated roads to ease vehicle flow. Critics also point to the substantial costs and logistical challenges of retrofitting existing, often unplanned, urban infrastructure to be truly pedestrian-friendly. While footpaths themselves are relatively inexpensive (approx. ₹10 lakh per km compared to ₹10 crore per km for a road), comprehensive urban redesign involves much more. Moreover, even where pedestrian infrastructure exists, it often suffers from encroachment by vendors, parked vehicles, or poor maintenance, rendering it unusable. Some argue that the immediate focus should rather be on enhancing public transportation systems—like metro and bus networks—and improving last-mile connectivity, which would indirectly benefit pedestrians by reducing the overall need for private vehicles. The underlying car-centric planning philosophy, which has historically allocated over 70% of road space to vehicles despite over 40% of trips being on foot, presents a significant cultural and political hurdle to overcome.

Key facts

  • Over 40% of daily trips in Indian cities are made on foot, yet more than 70% of road space is allocated to vehicles.
  • Improved footpaths in Chennai between 2013-2019 led to an estimated annual reduction of 4,200 to 12,000 tonnes of CO2-equivalent emissions.
  • The cost of constructing a well-designed footpath is approximately ₹10 lakh per km, significantly less than the ₹10 crore per km for a modern road.

The bottom line

The debate over prioritizing pedestrian mobility in Indian cities like Gurugram is a complex one, balancing the urgent need for efficient vehicular movement with the fundamental right to safe, healthy, and equitable urban spaces. While the environmental, health, and economic benefits of walkable cities are well-documented, the challenges of re-engineering existing infrastructure and shifting long-held planning paradigms are considerable. Ultimately, finding a sustainable path forward will likely require a multi-faceted approach that integrates robust public transport, smart urban design, effective enforcement, and a renewed commitment to people-centric urban development.
