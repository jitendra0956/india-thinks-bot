New UPI Merchant Charge: Fair for Small Businesses?

September 16, 2026 · 3 min read

The government and NPCI have introduced a 0.4% Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) on person-to-merchant (P2M) UPI transactions exceeding ₹2,000, effective October 15, 2026. This move, which follows nearly six years of zero-MDR on UPI, aims to ensure the long-term sustainability and growth of India's digital payments infrastructure. However, it has sparked a debate on its fairness, particularly for small businesses that have widely adopted UPI for its cost-free convenience.

Why people say yes

Proponents argue that the MDR is crucial for the continued development and maintenance of UPI's robust infrastructure. The revenue generated will be distributed among payment ecosystem participants, including banks and app providers, enabling investments in cybersecurity, innovation, and customer service. This is essential for UPI to remain a reliable and accessible payment method in the long run, as without a sustainable revenue model, the ecosystem's ability to invest in growth and resilience would be constrained.

The new UPI MDR of 0.4% for transactions above ₹2,000 is significantly lower compared to charges on other digital payment instruments. For instance, standard credit card MDRs typically range from 1.5% to 2.5%, and debit card MDRs are capped at up to 0.90%. This comparatively lower rate ensures that UPI remains the most affordable digital payment acceptance tool for merchants, even with the new charge.

A key aspect of the new framework is its emphasis on protecting small businesses and low-value transactions. Person-to-person (P2P) transactions remain free, and P2M transactions up to ₹2,000 also continue to have zero MDR, which accounts for over 95% of total P2M transaction volume. Furthermore, small merchants receiving up to ₹1 lakh per month through UPI QR codes under the Person-to-Person-Merchant (P2PM) framework will continue to enjoy zero MDR on all their transactions, directly safeguarding street vendors and neighborhood shops.

Why people say no

Critics express concern that the introduction of MDR, even if seemingly small, will add to the operational costs for businesses. Merchants will be required to pay 0.4% of the transaction value to their acquiring banks for P2M payments exceeding ₹2,000. While consumers are not directly charged, this added expense could squeeze the profit margins of businesses, especially those with high transaction volumes above the threshold.

Although micro-merchants with monthly UPI inflows up to ₹1 lakh are exempt, many small businesses, particularly those in metros and major cities, often exceed this threshold. Once they cross this limit, they transition into the standard P2M category and become subject to the MDR charges for larger transactions, potentially hindering their growth and increasing their financial burden as they scale up.

There is a concern that the new charge might subtly discourage merchants from accepting UPI for larger transactions, or even prompt them to consider splitting payments to avoid the MDR. While the government has advised banks to ensure merchants do not pass on the MDR cost to consumers, the economic reality for some small businesses might lead them to prefer cash for higher-value transactions, potentially slowing the momentum of digital payment adoption.

Key facts

  • A 0.4% Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) applies to person-to-merchant (P2M) UPI transactions above ₹2,000, effective October 15, 2026.
  • Transactions of ₹75,000 and above have the MDR capped at ₹300 per transaction.
  • Small merchants receiving up to ₹1 lakh per month through UPI QR codes are exempt from MDR on all transactions.

The bottom line

The introduction of MDR on higher-value UPI transactions marks a significant shift in India's digital payments landscape, moving from a zero-MDR regime to a more structured model. While designed to foster the long-term sustainability and growth of the UPI ecosystem and protect the vast majority of small-value and micro-merchant transactions, it simultaneously introduces a new cost component for businesses handling larger digital payments. The success of this policy will hinge on its ability to balance ecosystem funding with continued ease of adoption and minimal disruption for merchants across the country, especially as digital payments continue to evolve.
