India's Fuel Dilemma: Reintroducing E10 or Sticking with E20?

August 18, 2026 · 3 min read

India's Chief Economic Adviser recently sparked a nationwide debate by calling for the reintroduction of E10 petrol alongside the currently mandated E20 blend. This recommendation comes amid growing consumer concerns about the impact of E20 (petrol blended with 20% ethanol) on vehicle performance and longevity, particularly for older models. The move to E20, made mandatory from April 1, 2026, was aimed at reducing crude oil imports, cutting pollution, and boosting the agricultural sector. However, its rapid rollout has led to a complex discussion at the intersection of energy policy, automotive engineering, and consumer protection.

Why people say yes

Proponents of reintroducing E10 argue primarily for consumer choice and protection of existing vehicles. Many vehicles on Indian roads, especially those manufactured before April 2023, were designed for lower ethanol blends like E10, not E20. Forcing these older vehicles to run on E20 can lead to potential issues such as corrosion in fuel lines, damage to rubber seals, starting troubles, rough idling, and reduced mileage. Some experts suggest that E20's hygroscopic nature (its tendency to absorb moisture) can accelerate wear and tear on fuel system components, necessitating more frequent maintenance. A significant number of motorists have reported a drop in fuel efficiency and increased maintenance costs since the E20 mandate. Reintroducing E10 would offer a viable option for owners of these older vehicles, allowing them to continue using compatible fuel and mitigating potential damage.

Another key argument is the issue of fuel quality and potential contamination. Internal communications from major Indian automakers have reportedly raised concerns about high chloride and moisture content in E20 samples collected across various states, significantly exceeding permissible limits. Such contamination can be corrosive to auto parts and lead to immediate vehicle immobilization. Offering E10 alongside E20 could provide a more robust fuel option, especially if quality control concerns with E20 persist.

Finally, the call for E10 acknowledges that not all vehicles are E20-compatible, despite government assurances. While new vehicles are E20-compliant, there is no formal assessment by the government on the percentage of older vehicles compatible with E20. This lack of data, combined with widespread consumer complaints and even legal challenges, suggests a need for a more cautious approach to fuel policy, prioritizing consumer experience and the diverse vehicle fleet on Indian roads.

Why people say no

Opponents of reintroducing E10 emphasize the significant national benefits of the E20 programme. A primary driver for E20 is to enhance India's energy security by reducing dependence on imported crude oil, saving substantial foreign exchange. India has saved approximately ₹1.65 lakh crore in foreign exchange since 2014 due to ethanol blending. Lowering the blending target or reintroducing E10 as a widespread option could undermine these economic and strategic gains.

E20 also offers environmental advantages. Ethanol burns cleaner than conventional petrol, leading to reduced greenhouse gas emissions and improved air quality. E20 blending has cumulatively reduced CO2 emissions by approximately 832 lakh metric tonnes. The government aims for a cleaner transportation sector, and a higher ethanol blend is a step towards achieving long-term environmental sustainability and India's commitment to climate goals.

Furthermore, the ethanol blending programme supports the agricultural sector and rural economies by creating additional demand for crops like sugarcane, maize, and surplus food grains, which are used as feedstock for ethanol production. This provides an assured market for farmers and helps in clearing sugarcane arrears. While concerns about food-versus-fuel trade-offs exist, the government's stance is that only surplus food grains are diverted for ethanol production after meeting food security requirements. Reverting to lower blends could negatively impact the investments made in distillery capacity and the biofuel industry.

Key facts

  • India made E20 petrol mandatory nationwide from April 1, 2026, to reduce oil imports and pollution, achieving its 20% blending target five years early.
  • The Chief Economic Adviser recently called for E10 petrol to be available alongside E20, citing concerns for older vehicles.
  • Vehicles manufactured before April 2023 were primarily designed for lower ethanol blends, leading to potential compatibility issues with E20 like reduced mileage or engine wear.

The bottom line

The debate surrounding E10 and E20 in India highlights a critical balancing act between national energy security, environmental goals, and consumer interests. While E20 offers significant benefits in reducing dependence on foreign oil and lowering emissions, concerns about its impact on older vehicles and fuel quality are valid. The proposition of reintroducing E10 aims to address these consumer anxieties and safeguard the existing vehicle fleet, without necessarily abandoning the broader ethanol blending objectives. The ultimate decision will likely involve a careful consideration of these intertwined economic, environmental, and social factors.
