India Debates NEET-UG's Future: Two Stages, Computer-Based?

August 10, 2026 · 3 min read

The Union government recently informed the Supreme Court on August 5, 2026, that it is actively considering a significant change to the National Eligibility cum Entrance Test (Undergraduate), or NEET-UG. This potential shift to a two-stage, computer-based examination format has ignited a nationwide debate, as it could fundamentally alter the pathway for millions of students aspiring to pursue medical careers in India. The proposed reform aims to address various long-standing concerns regarding the current single-stage, pen-and-paper examination, but also raises new questions about accessibility and implementation challenges.

Why people say yes

Proponents argue that a two-stage examination process could significantly improve the fairness and effectiveness of the NEET-UG system. By introducing an initial screening stage, the exam could effectively filter out less serious candidates, allowing the subsequent, more rigorous stage to focus on truly dedicated and meritorious applicants, similar to the JEE (Main + Advanced) model. This structured approach could lead to a more streamlined evaluation process for the top contenders.

Furthermore, advocates suggest that breaking the exam into two stages might alleviate some of the immense pressure associated with a single, high-stakes examination day. Students would have a chance to experience an initial assessment, potentially reducing anxiety and allowing for more targeted preparation for the second, decisive stage. This could foster a more robust and resilient pool of candidates who are thoroughly tested over an extended period.

The move to a computer-based format is also seen as a crucial step towards modernizing the examination system. Computer-based tests (CBT) offer enhanced security measures, making them less susceptible to malpractices such as paper leaks, as digital security measures are often more robust. CBT can provide greater flexibility in scheduling, reduce logistical complexities, and enable faster and more transparent result processing, thereby increasing overall efficiency.

Why people say no

Conversely, a substantial number of students, parents, and educators express strong reservations about the proposed two-stage, computer-based format. A primary concern is the significant increase in financial and mental burden on students. Requiring candidates to appear for two separate examinations would entail additional travel, accommodation, and preparation costs, disproportionately affecting those from economically weaker sections and remote areas who already struggle with the existing single-stage format.

Another major challenge highlighted is the pervasive digital divide across India. While urban centers might have adequate access to computers and reliable internet, many students in rural and semi-urban areas lack the necessary infrastructure or familiarity with computer-based testing. This disparity could create an unfair playing field, disadvantaging a large segment of aspiring doctors and potentially exacerbating existing inequalities in access to quality education.

Moreover, the technical challenges inherent in conducting a massive, nationwide computer-based exam cannot be overlooked. Concerns about potential technical glitches, internet connectivity failures, power outages, server crashes, and system malfunctions during the examination are significant. Such disruptions could severely impact students' performance, introduce unfairness, and undermine the credibility of the entire testing process.

Key facts

  • NEET-UG is currently a single-stage, pen-and-paper examination conducted nationwide.
  • Computer-based testing can offer benefits like improved security, faster results, and logistical ease.
  • Challenges with computer-based testing include the digital divide, risk of technical failures, and the potential for a new level of inequality.

The bottom line

The proposal to transition NEET-UG to a two-stage, computer-based examination is a complex issue with valid arguments on both sides. While proponents envision a more secure, efficient, and thorough assessment system, critics fear increased burden, exacerbated digital inequality, and technical complications. As the Centre considers this significant reform, the nation watches closely, debating how best to ensure a fair and equitable path to medical education for all aspiring doctors.
