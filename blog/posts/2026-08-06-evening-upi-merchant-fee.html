India Debates UPI Fees: The Future of Digital Payments

August 06, 2026 · 3 min read

On August 5, 2026, a new government Bill introduced in Lok Sabha ignited a significant national discussion regarding the potential introduction of charges on Unified Payments Interface (UPI) transactions, specifically focusing on amounts exceeding ₹2,000. This legislative move, which seeks to amend the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007, has brought to the forefront a long-standing debate about the financial sustainability of India's massively successful digital payments ecosystem against the principle of free public utility. The proposed changes do not immediately impose fees but empower the government to notify which electronic payment modes may attract Merchant Discount Rate (MDR), setting the stage for a potential policy shift that could redefine how millions transact daily.

Why people say yes

Proponents of introducing a merchant fee argue it's crucial for the long-term sustainability and growth of India's digital payments infrastructure. The operation and maintenance of the vast UPI ecosystem incur substantial costs, estimated to be thousands of crores annually. Without a direct revenue stream like MDR, payment service providers (PSPs) and banks struggle to recover these expenditures and invest in necessary technological upgrades, cybersecurity measures, and further innovation. The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) Governor Sanjay Malhotra recently reiterated that "someone has to pay the cost" of maintaining this infrastructure. Furthermore, by targeting transactions above ₹2,000, which constitute a small percentage of transaction volume but a significant portion of the total value, the proposed fees aim to monetize UPI without impacting the vast majority of small, everyday transactions that drive financial inclusion. This approach allows the ecosystem to become self-reliant, reducing reliance on government subsidies and fostering a more robust, market-driven development.

Why people say no

Conversely, critics express significant concerns that introducing merchant fees, even on high-value transactions, could undermine UPI's success and negatively impact the economy. While the charges are intended for merchants, there's a strong possibility that businesses might pass these costs on to consumers, leading to increased prices for goods and services. This could make digital payments less attractive, potentially pushing users back towards cash transactions, thereby reversing the gains made in digital adoption and financial transparency. Many argue that UPI, being a public digital good, should continue to be entirely free, with its operational costs absorbed by the government or the RBI, which reportedly has sufficient surplus funds. They contend that the government already benefits significantly from the increased tax compliance and reduced costs associated with a less-cash economy, outweighing the cost of subsidizing UPI. Introducing complexity and charges could erode the trust and simplicity that have been cornerstones of UPI's widespread acceptance.

Key facts

  • In July 2026, India's UPI processed a record 23.66 billion transactions, totaling Rs. 29.88 lakh crore (approximately US$313.47 billion).
  • Transactions exceeding ₹2,000 account for only about 4-5% of total UPI transaction volumes but represent a substantial 65-70% of the total transaction value.
  • Currently, bank-to-bank UPI transactions are free of MDR for merchants; however, an interchange fee of up to 1.1% already applies to PPI (Prepaid Payment Instrument) based UPI merchant transactions above ₹2,000, borne by the merchant.

The bottom line

The debate over UPI charges above ₹2,000 highlights a critical juncture for India's digital payments journey. Balancing the need for a financially sustainable ecosystem that can continue to innovate and expand with the objective of keeping digital payments accessible and affordable for all remains the central challenge. The proposed legislative changes initiate a framework for potential monetization, with the final details on rates, thresholds, and exemptions yet to be determined by the government. The outcome will significantly shape the future trajectory of digital transactions in India, impacting millions of users and businesses alike.
