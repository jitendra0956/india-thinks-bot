Decoding India's Packaged Food Debate: To Warn or Not To Warn?

September 07, 2026 · 3 min read

India is currently grappling with a critical public health debate: should all packaged food products prominently display front-of-pack (FOP) warning labels? This discussion gained significant momentum recently as the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) proposed a system of red, hexagonal warning labels for packaged foods high in added sugar, salt, and fat. This move follows sustained pressure from the Supreme Court and aligns with calls from nutrition influencers like Revant Himatsingka, who has long advocated for clearer, more transparent food labeling to combat misleading marketing and rising non-communicable diseases.

Why people say yes

Proponents argue that mandatory FOP warning labels are a vital public health intervention. They believe such labels empower consumers to make healthier food choices quickly and easily, without needing to decipher complex nutritional tables on the back of packaging. By clearly indicating products high in critical nutrients like sugar, salt, and saturated fat, these labels can significantly reduce the intake of unhealthy foods, thereby combating the escalating burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) such as obesity, diabetes, and heart conditions in India. International examples, such as Chile's FOP warning labels, demonstrate tangible success, with studies showing a notable decrease in the purchase of unhealthy items like sugary drinks. Furthermore, supporters suggest that a strong FOPL system incentivizes food manufacturers to reformulate their products to be healthier, fostering a more health-conscious food industry.

Why people say no

On the other hand, critics and some industry bodies express reservations about mandatory FOP warning labels. One primary concern is that such labels, particularly simplified warnings, might over-simplify the complex nuances of nutrition, potentially creating unnecessary "food fear" among consumers rather than fostering a balanced understanding of diet. Some industry stakeholders also raise concerns about the potential economic burden on small businesses, necessitating significant investment in packaging overhauls and product reformulation. More specifically, public health advocates themselves have voiced criticism over the FSSAI's proposed two-phase implementation, which initially targets products high in *two or more* harmful nutrients. They argue this threshold is too weak, allowing many products high in just one critical nutrient to escape scrutiny, thereby undermining the effectiveness of the labels. They advocate for a stricter, single-nutrient threshold consistent with international best practices.

Key facts

  • The FSSAI has proposed red hexagonal warning labels for packaged foods high in added fat, sugar, and salt.
  • The proposed system involves a two-phase implementation, starting with products high in two or more specified nutrients.
  • The Supreme Court of India has actively pushed the FSSAI to finalize these FOPL regulations, emphasizing public health.
  • Nutrition influencer Revant Himatsingka has been a key voice advocating for clearer front-of-pack labels in India.

The bottom line

The debate over front-of-pack warning labels in India underscores a broader societal challenge: balancing consumer information with industry realities and effective public health policy. While there is a consensus on the need for clearer nutritional guidance, the specifics of implementation, including the design, thresholds, and scope of these labels, remain points of contention. As India continues to address the rising tide of lifestyle diseases, the final shape of its FOPL policy will have significant implications for both public health and the packaged food industry.
