Pune Incident Sparks Debate: Should Restaurants Face Harsher Hygiene Penalties?

September 07, 2026 · 3 min read

The recent viral video from Pune showing a customer finding a rubber band in their food on September 7, 2026, has once again highlighted deep-seated concerns over food hygiene in Indian restaurants. This incident has swiftly reignited public debate and regulatory scrutiny, prompting many to question the adequacy of current penalties for hygiene violations. As consumer awareness grows and social media amplifies such lapses, the nation is grappling with a critical question: should restaurants face harsher penalties for failing to meet food safety standards?

Why people say yes

Stricter penalties are seen as vital to safeguard public health. With an estimated 100 million foodborne illnesses and 120,000 deaths annually in India, advocates argue current regulations inadequately deter unsafe practices. Harsher punishments, including higher fines and more frequent license suspensions, could compel establishments to prioritize hygiene, reducing food contamination and related health issues.

Furthermore, increased penalties could act as a stronger deterrent against negligence. When financial and operational consequences are severe, restaurants would be more incentivized to invest in proper training, infrastructure, and regular quality checks. This proactive approach would ensure consistently higher standards of food preparation and service across the industry.

Ultimately, robust enforcement builds consumer trust. Surveys indicate a significant trust deficit, with only 11% of consumers trusting their local Food & Drug Administration. An 2025 PwC survey showed 84% of Indian consumers are concerned about food safety. Demonstrating a firm stance against violations can reassure diners, encouraging them to eat out more frequently and without apprehension.

Why people say no

Conversely, some argue that imposing harsher penalties could disproportionately burden small and medium-sized restaurants. These businesses often operate on thin profit margins and may struggle to absorb costs for extensive upgrades, compliance, and potential fines or temporary closures. Such measures could lead to numerous closures, job losses, and reduced culinary diversity.

Another concern is that increased operating costs due to stricter compliance requirements would inevitably be passed on to consumers. Restaurants might need to invest significantly in new equipment, staff training, and compliance systems. These additional expenses could lead to higher menu prices, making dining out less affordable for the average Indian consumer and potentially hurting the industry.

Finally, opponents suggest that existing laws are comprehensive but suffer from enforcement issues rather than leniency. India's Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006, already provides substantial penalties, including fines up to ₹5 lakh for substandard food and up to life imprisonment for severe adulteration. The real challenge lies in consistent inspections and adequate staffing, not just increasing penalty severity.

Key facts

  • The Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006, is India's primary food safety law.
  • FSSAI can impose fines up to ₹5 lakh for substandard food and up to ₹10 lakh with life imprisonment for fatal adulteration.
  • Nearly one in six food samples tested in FY26 failed safety and quality checks nationwide.

The bottom line

The debate over harsher penalties for restaurant hygiene touches upon the delicate balance between public safety and business viability. While recent incidents underscore the urgent need for improved food safety, the path forward requires careful consideration of both consumer protection and the economic realities faced by food businesses. Striking the right balance is crucial for a safe and sustainable food industry.
