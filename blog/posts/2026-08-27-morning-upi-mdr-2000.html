The UPI MDR Debate: Should High-Value Payments Be Charged?

August 27, 2026 · 3 min read

India's Unified Payments Interface (UPI) has revolutionized digital payments, becoming a cornerstone of the country's economy. Recent developments, however, signal a potential shift in its operational model. In August 2026, the Indian Parliament passed the Taxation and Other Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026, which received the President's assent on August 17, 2026. This amendment to the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007, removes the legal mandate for zero Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) on UPI and RuPay debit card transactions, thereby enabling the government to notify specific charges in the future. This move has sparked a national debate: should MDR be levied on UPI payments exceeding ₹2,000, and what are the implications for India's digital payments landscape?

Why people say yes

Proponents argue that introducing MDR on high-value UPI transactions is crucial for the long-term sustainability and growth of the digital payments ecosystem. Banks and payment service providers incur substantial costs in building, maintaining, and securing the robust UPI infrastructure, including servers, cybersecurity measures, and fraud detection systems. MDR would provide a much-needed revenue stream to cover these operational expenses, reducing the reliance on government subsidies.

Furthermore, this revenue can be reinvested into enhancing technology, fostering innovation, and expanding the reach of digital payment services, particularly in underserved areas. Monetizing transactions could give payment service providers greater flexibility to invest in technology and services, strengthening their business models. The move could also level the playing field with other digital payment methods like credit and debit cards, which already attract MDR, ensuring fair competition across the payments industry.

Finally, industry experts highlight that while UPI has seen exponential growth, the cost of running such a massive public utility must be borne by someone. The RBI Governor has stated that "someone has to pay the cost" to ensure the public infrastructure continues to strengthen and evolve. A targeted MDR on higher-value merchant transactions is seen as a way to achieve this without burdening small merchants or consumers for everyday transactions.

Why people say no

Critics express concerns about the potential burden on merchants, despite assurances that consumers and small businesses will be exempt. While the proposed MDR is expected to apply primarily to large merchants for transactions above ₹2,000, these businesses might absorb the additional cost or, in some cases, factor it into their pricing, indirectly affecting consumers. Small retailers and local businesses, even if exempt, could still face indirect impacts through changes in the overall payment ecosystem.

Opponents also worry that introducing any charge, even if selective, could potentially hinder the remarkable digital adoption that UPI has achieved. The zero-MDR policy was instrumental in making UPI a mass payment system, deeply embedded in everyday Indian payments. Any deviation from this free model, even for high-value transactions, could send mixed signals and potentially slow down the momentum of digital payments, especially if merchants feel incentivized to push for other payment methods.

Moreover, the government has historically championed free UPI transactions to promote financial inclusion and a less-cash economy, even providing subsidies to support the ecosystem. Critics argue that moving away from this core principle, even with good intentions, might undermine the very foundation upon which UPI's success was built. They emphasize that UPI is a public good, and its financing should continue through mechanisms that do not place direct costs on transactions.

Key facts

  • The Taxation and Other Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026, passed by Parliament and assented by the President in August 2026, removed the legal provision mandating zero MDR on UPI and RuPay debit card transactions.
  • The proposal suggests MDR would apply to Payment-to-Merchant (P2M) transactions exceeding ₹2,000, primarily targeting large merchants, while Person-to-Person (P2P) and small merchant transactions are intended to remain free.
  • Discussions around proposed MDR rates for high-value transactions range between 0.25% and 0.5%, which is notably lower than typical credit card MDRs.

The bottom line

The debate surrounding MDR on high-value UPI payments highlights a critical balancing act: ensuring the long-term financial viability of India's leading digital payment system while preserving its accessibility and affordability. As the government prepares to finalize the framework, stakeholders across the economy are keenly watching how this policy shift will shape the future of digital transactions in India, balancing ecosystem sustainability with continued user adoption.
