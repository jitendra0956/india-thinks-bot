The UPI Safety Debate: Should Apps Prompt for Suspicious Transactions?

August 03, 2026 · 3 min read

Indian banks have proposed a significant new measure to combat the surging tide of digital payment fraud: a 'Yes-No' confirmation prompt for high-risk UPI transactions. Announced on August 2, 2026, this initiative seeks to introduce a vital layer of security without imposing a blanket delay on all transactions. As India's digital payment ecosystem continues its rapid expansion, protecting users from increasingly sophisticated scams has become paramount, sparking a nationwide debate on balancing security with the seamless convenience UPI is known for.

Why people say yes

Proponents argue that a mandatory 'Yes-No' prompt for suspicious transactions offers a crucial defense against authorized push-payment frauds, where users are tricked into initiating payments themselves. These scams, often involving social engineering and collect request abuse, represent a significant portion of fraud losses. A confirmation prompt provides a critical pause, allowing users a moment to re-evaluate the transaction and potentially prevent financial loss before it's too late. It empowers users with direct control over their funds at a high-risk juncture.

This additional security layer is seen as a necessary evolution in safeguarding the UPI ecosystem. With digital payment fraud incidents climbing significantly, an extra step for flagged transactions ensures that enhanced protection is applied precisely where it's needed most. It builds greater trust in the system by demonstrating a proactive approach to user safety, especially as fraudsters constantly adapt their methods to bypass existing security measures. The prompt helps mitigate the rising financial impact on individuals and the broader economy.

Furthermore, by specifically targeting transactions deemed 'high-risk' – such as payments to new or unknown beneficiaries, or those made at unusual hours – this measure avoids penalizing routine, low-risk payments. This focused approach ensures that the convenience of UPI for everyday transactions remains largely unaffected. It offers a smart compromise between robust security and maintaining the speed and efficiency that have made UPI a cornerstone of India's digital economy. If no response is given, the payment would be held for one hour, providing a cooling-off period.

Why people say no

Critics of the proposal express concern that introducing an additional 'Yes-No' prompt, even for suspicious transactions, could introduce unnecessary friction into the UPI payment process. UPI's success is largely attributed to its instant, hassle-free nature. Any extra step, however minor, could slow down transactions and undermine the very convenience that millions of Indians rely on daily, potentially leading to user frustration and a less fluid payment experience. This could especially impact quick, on-the-go payments at merchant outlets.

The disruption to user experience is another major concern. While the intention is to target only suspicious transactions, the algorithm's accuracy is key. An overly sensitive system could frequently flag legitimate transactions, forcing users to repeatedly confirm payments that are perfectly normal for them. Such frequent interruptions could lead to 'prompt fatigue,' where users might reflexively confirm transactions without proper review, defeating the security purpose of the prompt and adding an irritating step to their daily financial routines.

Some argue that existing security measures, such as the UPI PIN and stricter two-factor authentication (2FA) already in place, are sufficient. The RBI and NPCI have continuously enhanced security requirements, including device binding and transaction limits. Adding another prompt might be perceived as redundant for many users, particularly those who are already careful with their transactions or primarily make low-value payments. While fraud is a serious issue, a blanket new prompt for 'suspicious' activity might not be the most efficient solution for an ecosystem that thrives on speed and simplicity.

Key facts

  • UPI fraud incidents in India have seen a significant increase, with reported cases climbing from 2.6 lakh in 2021 to 28 lakh in 2025, and the total value involved rising from ₹551 crore to ₹22,931 crore in the same period.
  • The proposed 'Yes-No' confirmation prompt is specifically for high-risk peer-to-peer (P2P) UPI transactions, triggered by unusual activity such as payments to new beneficiaries or at odd hours, and serves as an alternative to a general payment delay.
  • Existing security measures by the RBI and NPCI include device binding, mandatory UPI PINs, daily transaction limits, and stricter two-factor authentication (2FA) for digital payments, all aimed at enhancing user protection and combating social engineering scams.

The bottom line

The debate over requiring a 'Yes-No' prompt for suspicious UPI transactions highlights the ongoing tension between security and user convenience in India's booming digital payments landscape. While the proposal aims to curb substantial fraud losses and protect users, concerns remain about potential friction and disruptions to the seamless experience that has driven UPI's massive adoption. As the Reserve Bank of India reviews this proposal, the challenge lies in finding a solution that effectively thwarts fraudsters without compromising the efficiency and accessibility that define the success of UPI for millions of users.
